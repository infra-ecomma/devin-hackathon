Tellurion.

A live instrument that draws a project as an orrery: the repo at the centre, its products as planets, their features as moons in orbit, and your standing tools, processes and workflows as the belt around them. It watches and never touches. It never writes to anything it draws, and every body on the plate is a count of a record that already exists: nothing on screen is interpolated, estimated, or projected. If the drawing ever shows a state the record cannot support, the instrument is lying and that outranks every other bug.

Purpose · why this exists

You cannot glance at a repository and tell whether it is moving.

A repo answers "what is in here" and never "what is happening". Git log is a list of edits, a task board is a list of intentions, and neither says which products exist, which of their features are actually signed off, or whether anything moved today. Tellurion draws that in one picture you can read in a glance from across the room, and it earns the right to be glanced at by never showing anything the record cannot support.

The problem

The first build drew one session's file edits, and the plate came back almost empty.

On 2026-08-24 the first version (commit 3c1f4815) visualised the artifacts of a single Claude session: six artifact-type shells and little else. Wassim's verdict was "really far from what I expected" and "so damn ugly", and he handed the project to Fable the same day. The diagnosis was not the drawing, it was the data scope: an instrument that shows one session's file churn has nothing to show, because a session is not what a project is made of. The binding re-scope was that the orrery carries products, features, tools, processes and workflows, and the spine carries products, features and milestones.

Who it is for
  • The operator, at a glance — one screen that answers whether the estate moved today, without reading anything
  • The operator, mid-decision — which product is furthest along, what is unsigned, what a milestone is still waiting on
  • A session picking up cold — the shape of a project before reading a single file
What "done" means

Done is that a person opens the URL on the machine they are sitting at, sees the project they are working on, and can name without clicking: which products exist, which of their features are open, claimed, verified or accepted, which milestones have been earned, and whether anything is happening right now. Files existing is not done. A running port is not done either: the kill criterion is explicit in the spec — if it runs and the operator stops glancing at it within a week, it is decoration and gets deleted rather than maintained.

Strategy

Draw the record, never the intention, and let form carry the taxonomy.

Two bets hold the design up. The first is that every number on screen must be a count of a graph that already exists, so the instrument can never flatter the project: the acceptance walk fails on any disagreement between the drawing and the data. The second is that every body indicates its kind by form alone, so the taxonomy survives being read at 6px and in peripheral vision, which is the only way a glanceable instrument can work.

BET 1

Counts, not estimates.

Everything drawn is mined from the repo into a graph and counted. Nothing is interpolated and nothing is projected, so a plate that looks empty means the record is empty, which is information rather than a rendering bug.

BET 2

Form is the legend.

Planet, moon, rim tick, belt square, comet, arc. Kind is readable without a key, so the plate stays legible when it is a glance on a second monitor rather than a document being studied.

BET 3

Lime means now, and nothing else.

Exactly one colour is reserved for live activity. Everything finished is drawn in full and goes quiet. Finished work never disappears, because the accumulated picture is the point.

BET 4

Read-only, permanently.

It observes the record and does not edit it. That is a non-goal in the spec rather than a current limitation, and it is what makes the instrument safe to leave running against a live repo.

How success is judged

The spec states the false-positive test directly: if the operator cannot tell a product's state from the plate without clicking, the encoding has failed; if a body's kind is not readable from its form alone, the taxonomy has failed; and if the replay ever shows a state the record cannot support, the instrument is lying and must be fixed before anything else.

Not a dashboard. No charts, no time series, no burndown. It is not a project manager, it does not write, and its graph is a deliberate mined snapshot rather than a filesystem guess. Those are non-goals, not gaps.
How it works

A read-only server mines a graph, serves one page, and streams what changes.

A Node server scans the watched repo at boot, holds the resulting graph in memory, and serves a single page that draws it. Three read-only feeds (a transcript tail, a per-directory watcher, and a git poll) reduce into pulses that are attributed onto bodies already on the plate. The browser and the server run the same reducer, so the page grows the same world the server does rather than re-fetching it.

Boot, measured

The scan is a boot-time pass over the watched tree rather than a background job, and it is quick enough that starting the instrument is not a wait: on Forge a tree of roughly 17,000 files resolves in under 100ms, with the per-directory watchers established in the same pass. What it resolves to — how many products, features and milestones appear — is a property of the repository on the plate rather than of the instrument, so no such count is quoted here.

What it draws, and what it leaves out

The bench appears only where a project has used it.

The standing kit is the tools, processes and workflows the operator carries between projects, and it used to be painted on every project from the first second whether or not that project had ever touched one of them, so a plate spent most of its space on things the operator had never used. Usage is recorded per project in .tellurion/usage.json so it survives a restart, and a Bench: used / all toggle shows the whole kit on demand. With nothing used, neither band is drawn and the product region expands to take the room.

The plan lifecycle

A spine you can edit, checked against the repo.

Ruled by the operator on 2026-08-29 (ADR-0134, extended the same day: inception is the birth for a new project). One: /inception. A new project's intake asks whether it is watched; the spec stage then writes Products and Build story sections into the spec in a fixed shape, and approving the spec births .tellurion/plan.json in the same beat — a deterministic parse of the approved sections, every product, home, phase, step and binding tracing to a line the operator read, all steps honestly planned. The project is on the map from its first minute. Two: the Initialize button. On an existing project with no plan, the spine's empty state offers Draft it with the instrument: the server spawns the project's own harness (claude -p, subscription auth, no API keys) loose in the repo, and that agent reads the real tree and writes .tellurion/plan.json itself. Judgment over strict rules: a missing product spec does not mean a missing product. The click answers in milliseconds; the plan lands when the file lands, usually inside two minutes, and the editor opens on it for the read-and-fix pass. A draft never paves an existing declaration without force. Three: init-tbk on an existing repo — the bootstrapper asks at its decision page whether this project's plan lives in Tellurion, proposes a spine from the bootstrapper's own locked product set, and writes the file at birth when approved; inside an inception run it sees the plan already born and skips. Four: by hand — the file, or the panel's Edit plan editor (products with home paths, phases, steps, statuses, produces bindings), which is reachable in every state, not just the empty one.

After every write the instrument answers "does the spine still connect to the project?" deterministically: a product's declared home exists in the repo, else a distinctive name word matches a top-level entry, else the report says the product does not connect. For those products the panel offers Propose connections: the harness reads the real tree and proposes homes, the filesystem vetoes any path that does not exist, and the operator accepts; nothing is applied silently. A connected home is load bearing: work under that path lights the planet even when the words never match.

The sign-off vocabulary

Three tiers, three different parties, and only the third moves the counter. A plan file can grant no more than the first.

  • claimed — the builder says it is done. From the plan file.
  • verified — a judge passed it. From .tellurion/verdicts.json; a row without a pass and a name is not a verdict.
  • accepted — the operator accepted it. Only this tier counts a plan step as done.

The model · ratified 2026-08-28

A project is the hub, its products are planets, and their features are moons.

The hierarchy was ratified against a ten-frame walkthrough of the real Maximus Desktop repo, built specifically to test whether the model was shared before any more of it was built. The operator chose project mode as the view to build; the estate view is parked, not discarded.

The test that separates a product from a feature
  • Could you stop building it without stopping the others? Creative Library, yes. Multi-select attach, no.
  • Would a person name it when saying what they use? Creative Library, yes. Multi-select attach, no.

Naming follows from this, and the test is stricter than “use a noun”. A feature is a part you could draw a box around and point at, so its name reads naturally after the word the: the plan spine, the render farm, the dispatch rail. An activity noun built out of the work’s own verb fails that test — “Discover every project” becoming “Project discovery” is the same step wearing a costume, and on the glass it reads as a worklog exactly as the raw verb did. When a name fails, name the thing that does the work instead: not “project discovery” but “the project index”. A feature also normally gathers several steps; one feature per step means the layer is carrying no information, and either the features are too small or the steps are written at capability size.

The spine is the plan, led by product

One section per product, opening to its features, and a feature opening to the steps that build it.

The spine was built from the plan’s phases, which is how work was sequenced rather than what the project is. Grouping by product fixed half of it. The other half was that a “feature” was a plan step under a different name: the reducer pushed every step into a list called features carrying the step’s own title, so a product opened onto a wall of verb sentences and the word feature was a display label. Features are now declared on the product in plan.json and a step names the one it builds, so a feature can be listed before any work on it exists — which is what a plan is for — and the steps sit one level down, closed, inside the feature they belong to. Sign-off moved with them: custody is addressed to the feature, and a feature with no verdict of its own inherits one when every step under it was passed, so nothing already signed was voided. Directories the instrument discovered on its own no longer sit in the same list wearing the same styling as declared products, which is what made the header’s product count and the spine’s list disagree.

Zoom is part of the model

The same object is a planet in one view and the hub in another, and the instrument has to say which.

Maximus Desktop is a planet in the estate view, sitting beside Shakeeb and Sentinel, and it is the hub in its own view. Both are correct, the way a city is a dot on one map and the whole of another. It is only correct if the view names its own zoom, because otherwise "planet" means two different things depending on which page you opened. Stating the zoom is part of the model, not chrome.

What project mode actually does

With a declared plan, the ladder is already what the code builds.

.tellurion/plan.json declares the products, and applyPlan builds one planet per declared product and one moon per plan step, joined by the step's produces.of. A guard then stops a directory or a file inventing anything alongside the declaration. Verified live on this repo the same day: the four declared products are the four planets drawn. Directories and files only grow the graph as a fallback, for a project that has declared no plan at all.

Corrected 2026-08-28, same day, before you read this. The first version of this document said project mode does not implement the model, and that genesis grows planets from directories full stop. That was taken from older prose in the spec rather than from the code, and it was wrong. Reading the reducer and the live graph is what corrected it.
The real defect, found by checking that claim

A typo in the plan silently invented a product, and it was drawn as a real one.

A step may name the product it produces. If that name was never declared, the parser kept the step anyway, which is right, and invented the product, which was not. On this repo's own plan, five steps named the-plan and the-plate while the declared ids were plan and plate, so the plate carried six product planets against four in the file and nothing on screen said which two were a typo. Fixed the same day: the reference still survives, the invention is drawn as a dashed dormant ghost captioned "not declared in products; named by N steps", it is skipped by the tier rollup so it can never wear a badge it did not earn, and it is counted as neither a product nor a project.

The law of light · operator ruling, 2026-08-30

A body exists because work started, and it shines because a check passed.

This amends the drawing rule above. A declared product or feature is the plan; the sky draws only the ones whose work has started (a first commit, a branch, or an agent mission touching it). A declared but untouched item stays a dashed row in the spine and does not exist as a body. applyPlan's planet-per-declared-product draw predates this ruling and does not obey it yet, recorded here the same way the reducer note on the components tab records code behind model.

Verification light comes in two levels, and the operator named this one of Tellurion's most important features:

  • Feature (moon): in progress is a dashed ember silhouette; a recorded check passing (exit 0 at HEAD) flips it to a solid body with a halo, a change in kind, never a brightness tweak. Stale evidence decays the halo to dotted amber; a failing check draws a broken red arc. No hand can set any of these.
  • Product (planet): while features are mixed it wears no ring of any kind; the verified fraction prints in its label. When every feature is verified at HEAD and the product's own end-to-end walk passes, a verification shade blooms around the whole system, the band ring appears where none existed, and the product's orbit line lights. If anything falls back out of verified, the shade fades and the band withdraws to a dotted ring.
One law, four skins · operator ruling, 2026-08-30

Ratified same day, later amended with the default: the instrument ships two styles in two themes, toggled in the product, and Rustic Light is the default skin. Futuristic is the AI-made constellation look (dark space, or Fog and Deep Navy in light, as on the live instrument); Rustic is the engraved star-atlas (cream on a night plate in dark, sepia ink on parchment in light). A skin changes token values and typefaces (Orbitron with JetBrains Mono in Futuristic, Cinzel with Courier Prime in Rustic), never geometry or state meaning, so spatial memory and the law of light survive the toggle. Verification green is a role with a per-skin value, and nothing else in a skin may wear it.

The cue itself was then sized to its importance, locked from mockup comparison: moon radius floor 5px in every skin; a verified moon keeps its category fill under a 32% verification-green wash with a bold 1.8px halo (the wash survives staleness while the halo decays to dotted amber); and the product cue follows Wassim's sharper flip, no ring to ring rather than ring to bigger ring: a partial product wears no ring geometry at all, and full verification arrives as the shade, a 3.2px band with outer hairline, and the lit orbit together. The full visual grammar, one formula per glyph, all four skins on the same sample sky, and the cue comparison live on the reimagining canvas.
